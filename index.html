Clinic Desk

The reception desk and doctor's dashboard for Dr. Anam's Dental Clinic, my father's new practice. I planned, designed, built, secured and launched it on my own, and it costs ₹0 a month to run.

The brief

A new clinic, a paper register, and no budget for software.

The problem

My parents were opening a dental clinic. With a paper register, follow-ups get forgotten, missed appointments go unnoticed, and the doctor's notes live in a notebook nobody else should read.

Clinic software charges a monthly fee and is built for hospitals. This clinic needed one simple screen for the front desk, one for the doctors, and nothing it would pay for every month.

Usable on day one Private medical notes Free to run Works on a phone

Who uses it

  • The receptionist registers patients, books visits, marks arrivals and works through the follow-up call list.

  • The doctors (my parents, sharing one login) see who is due, mark treatments, write private notes and set follow-ups.

  • The reception PC is a modest Dell i3 with a hard drive, so the app had to stay small and fast.

How I built it

Six steps, from scope to go-live.

Each step ended with something that worked and was committed to Git, so nothing was built on guesswork.

  1. 01

    Step 1 · 19 Nov

    Cut the scope to what a small clinic uses

    An earlier attempt of mine was overbuilt. I kept its good ideas and rebuilt around what a one-receptionist clinic does every day.

    • Patients, visits, follow-ups and doctor's notes: nothing else
    • Clinic rules written down first: Mon to Sat, last booking 6:30 PM

    Design every screen before the database

    I built all the screens in React and Tailwind on top of sample data, so the flow could be clicked through and changed cheaply before anything was stored.

    • Branding matched to the clinic's website: ivory, espresso and bronze
    • One landing page with two doors: Doctor and Reception desk
  3. 03

    Step 3 · 20 Nov

    Lock down the database, then prove it

    Patient data sits in Postgres on Supabase. The security rules live in the database itself, not just in the screens, so a hidden button can't be worked around.

    • Row Level Security on every table
    • Clinic hours enforced by the database: no Sunday or 7 PM bookings
    • 74 automated tests run against a real Postgres before every change

  4. 04

    Step 4 · 20 Nov

    Two doors, real logins

    Each person signs in to their own portal. The receptionist can't get through the doctor's door, and even if someone forced the page open, the database would still refuse every doctor-only action.

    • Doctor sessions log out after 15 idle minutes
    • Sessions end when the browser closes, on a shared PC
    • Short usernames like reception instead of long emails
  5. 05

    Step 5 · 20 Nov

    Connect real data, updating live

    When reception marks a patient as arrived, the doctor's dashboard updates on its own. No refreshing, no shouting across the clinic.

    • Live updates on patients, visits and notes
    • Marking treated books the follow-up in the same step
    • Tested end to end on the live database, then wiped clean
  6. 06

    Step 6 · 21 Nov

    Go live for ₹0, then polish

    Deployed to Cloudflare with strict security headers, then refined after testing with my family: editing patients and bookings, an undo for the doctor, and a fix for tapping patients on phones.

    • Content Security Policy and HTTPS-only headers
    • Responsive layout checked at phone size
    • A go-live guide so the clinic can run it without me

What makes it different

The problems nobody asked me to solve.

Most of the work wasn't the screens. It was the details that decide whether a small clinic can trust the app with its patients.

One-click Excel backup

The free database plan has no automatic backups, so I built one. A single button saves every patient, visit and note into a tidy three-sheet workbook. The Excel library loads only when the button is pressed, keeping the app fast, and the dashboard nudges the doctor if a week passes without a backup.

Beat an internet provider's block

Halfway through, the app couldn't reach its database: some Indian internet providers block Supabase's addresses outright. I traced it to the provider, then built a small Cloudflare Worker that relays the traffic, so the clinic works on any connection.

Instead of a paid messaging service, one click opens WhatsApp with the reminder already written. Messages carry only name, date and time, never anything medical.

Small touches for the front desk

  • Age appears the moment a date of birth is typed
  • Sundays and after-hours times can't be booked
  • Family members can share one phone number
  • Medical alerts are one tap and show in red everywhere
  • Indian numbers accepted with +91, 0 or spaces
  • The doctor's follow-ups land in reception's call list

A sample-data mode lets me test, train staff and take screenshots without touching real records. It only works on my laptop and can never switch on in the live app. Every patient on this page comes from it.

Takeaways

What this project taught me.

  1. Cutting scope is a feature. Dropping two thirds of my first plan is why the clinic got a working app in three days.

  2. Security belongs in the database. Rules that live under the screens hold, even if someone gets clever with the browser.

  3. Real users find real bugs. A tap that failed only on phones turned up in family testing, never on my laptop.
